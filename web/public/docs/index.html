FeRun · Architecture · as of 8 November 2026

FeRun and FeOS

Two products on one technology: agent sandboxes, workspaces in which an LLM agent runs commands and tools for its user, often for days, and an isolated container runtime, a container as a service (CaaS) for customers' own images. Both run on the same unit, the pod: a Firecracker microVM with containers in it, which costs no memory while idle and is back in under a second.

FeRun the cluster component

ferund in replicas over one PostgreSQL. It knows the tenants, places pods on nodes and decides when they sleep. Only FeRun knows whether a pod is a sandbox or a service.

FeOS on the node

feosd, diskd and the guest. They run the pods: the microVMs, their disks, their network and their snapshots.

Inside a node

On Kubernetes, FeOS runs as the DaemonSet feos-node: one pod per node, with the containers diskd and feosd. The microVMs live in feosd's container. A planned stop suspends them, and the next feosd resumes them (ADR 0020). Under systemd the same processes run without the pod around them.

A pod's network

  • An IPv6 address from the node's prefix: one the network routes to the node (--pod-prefix), or a /96 that ferund draws from the cluster's ULA /64 (--pod-network mesh). Plus an IPv4 /32 from 169.254.16.0/20, only for leaving the node through masquerade.
  • A routed TAP device per pod, pinned to its addresses and named by the pod's 11-character ID. Proxy ARP answers for the IPv4 gateway 169.254.0.1.
  • Security groups as nftables chains per pod and direction in the table inet feos. Rules only allow, without order; ferund derives them from the deployments and sends them with a version per group (Who reaches whom).
  • Egress by name for a pod that restricts it: the resolver answers only its allowed names and puts their addresses into timed sets that its out chain admits.
  • DNS at 169.254.0.53 from feosd, with the environments' name tables. Metadata through Firecracker's MMDS v2.
  • Traffic records: flows from connection tracking, drops, DNS queries and server names, without eBPF and with nothing in the data path.

A request's path

A visitor opens a public environment's host under the apps zone, or a domain its tenant bound to it. ferund is not in the data path. It only supplies the table and wakes pods when asked.

CaaS on the sandbox engine

Environments are not a second system. Every pod of an environment is a row in the same table sandboxes as an agent's sandbox, and the same engine places, pauses and moves it (ADR 0018).

Who reaches whom

A tenant writes no firewall rule. What a deployment references, it may reach, and nothing else is open, not even within its own environment (ADR 0034). ferund derives the rules from the references, and every node enforces them by address in nftables. Within a cluster the address is the workload: a pod sends from its own address only, a node's /96 arrives only from the node that holds its WireGuard key, and traffic between nodes is encrypted.

What opens what

  • A reference opens the port it names: a variable from a service's port or an addon's output, a URL whose host is a service or an addon, a route (for the ingress), or uses for a call without a variable. By port name, so a changed number changes the rule with it. A workload's replicas reach each other on its ports.
  • Egress is a service's or a job's own: internet by default (global unicast, without private and special-purpose ranges), none, or {domains, blocks} with exceptions per block. feosd's resolver answers a restricted pod only its domains and puts their addresses into the pod's timed sets for their TTL, at least 5 minutes and at most a day; any other name gets REFUSED. While the guest still pulls images itself, a restricted pod may also reach its image's registry. Addons reach the internet.
  • A route's from lists the client blocks the ingress admits; other clients get 403. Without it, the route is open to everyone while its environment is public.
  • A sandbox or a tenant's job names at its create what of its environment it reaches: a list, all, or nothing, the default. PUT …/reaches changes a sandbox's in place, since the rules are its targets'; nothing moves. Nothing of the environment reaches a sandbox but through an attached service's port.
  • What the platform carries needs no rule: DNS, the metadata service, the node address 169.254.169.1 (traces, logs, HTTP bindings) and readiness checks over the port forward.

Identity and limits

  • No mTLS between services. The address already names the workload, and the node admits by it. A workload's certificate (ADR 0014) is for credentials without a password and for calls that leave the cluster, not for who reaches what.
  • A shared address. A CDN serves many names from one address: a pod allowed a.cdn.example reaches that address and may ask it for another name. The server name is recorded, not checked.
  • A routed cluster (every node with a prefix of the network's, no mesh) has no WireGuard, and no check that a source from another node's prefix came from that node: there, the network must filter (docs/security.md).

Auth at the ingress proposed

Proposed in ADR 0054, and none of it is built. Today a public environment's hosts are open to everyone, and a route's from is the only check at the ingress. Proposed is a switch on the environment: it admits anyone, or visitors, which is what a new environment does. Who a visitor is, and whether they may enter, is not FeRun's to know. An access provider the tenant names vouches for them, once per visit. After that the ingress decides by its own cookie, and neither ferund nor the provider is in a request's path. ferund serves no visitor at any point: it keeps the visits, for callers that authenticate. Where FeRun itself signs visitors in, a program of its own beside ferund does it.

Three providers, one challenge

The challenge is the one interface, and who answers it is the provider's kind. A provider is the tenant's, the switch is the environment's; an environment that admits visitors names one of its tenant's providers.

ProviderAnswers the challengeAdmitsFor
ferunferun-auth, with the installation's providerpeople who hold a grant of at least read on the environment; ferund checks the grant itselfa tenant's own people. The default: nothing to set up
oidcferun-auth, with any OpenID Connect issuer the tenant names: a client ID and a sealed client secret, the code flow with PKCEeveryone the issuer signs in, or those whose claims match: an email, its domain, a group in groupsan app with no sign-in of its own: the general OIDC proxy
tenantthe tenant's own service at its address, with its own session and whatever provider it useswhoever it accepts, by a rule only it knows, checked at that momenta platform on FeRun whose environments belong to projects of its own: FeCode admits a project's members
  • With tenant, FeRun signs nobody in. The tenant's service reads and accepts challenges through the API with the tenant's token. At each renewal ferund calls the same address and names the visit, and the service reads it and answers yes or no. It needs no signing key, FeRun keeps no list of its people, and nothing has to be kept in step between the two: no call tells FeRun that someone may no longer enter.
  • With ferun and oidc, the provider is ferun-auth, the OIDC proxy that comes with FeRun. It answers challenges through the same API as a tenant's service does.

ferun-auth, beside ferund

  • A program of its own, built from the same Go module and shipped in the same image as ferund, run beside it like the ingress and the sink. It has a host of its own, never a path of ferund's. That host carries the one redirect URI an issuer needs, whatever domains the environments have, and takes issuers' back-channel logouts.
  • Why not ferund. A sign-in page is open to anyone on the internet, and an issuer's address is a tenant's input that the proxy must fetch from. ferund holds the database and the nodes' port; its front door stays what it is: the API and the console, for tenants and operators.
  • What it may do at ferund, with a service token of its own kind: read and accept challenges of environments whose provider is ferun or oidc, read those providers' settings with their client secrets, and end visits by an issuer's sid. Nothing of tenants, deployments or nodes. For ferun, ferund looks up the grant itself, so the proxy cannot admit someone who holds none.
  • Issuers on the public internet only. It fetches from an issuer at global addresses alone, checked on the address it connects to: no network of the cluster, no private or link-local range. An operator names blocks as exceptions, for an issuer in a private network.
  • No store. A sign-in in flight lives in a sealed cookie on its host, and the long session is the issuer's. Instances need nothing from each other.
  • An installation without it serves tenant providers alone.

The visit

  • A visit is ferund's record that a provider admitted a subject to an environment until a time: the subject as the provider names it, what it said about them (an email, a name, groups), and the end the provider set, within the installation's bound.
  • The cookie is the ingress's, on the host the visitor opened and on no other: __Host-ferun_visit, HttpOnly. It holds a token ferund signed for 15 minutes, naming the visit, the environment and the subject. The ingress checks it with ferund's public key from the table, without a call, and takes the cookie out of the request before the pod sees it.
  • Renewal. When the token nears its end, the ingress asks ferund for the next over its connection, on any request, a script's too, so a page that stays open does not break.
  • Asked again every 15 minutes. At each renewal ferund puts the visit to its provider: for tenant a call to the provider's address, for ferun a look at the grant. A no ends the visit, so a removed member stays for 15 minutes at most, and a tenant has no call to end a visit with. A provider that does not answer has not said no: the visit goes on and is put to it again at the next renewal. An oidc visit is not asked about; it lives to its end or its issuer's logout.
  • Ending. A visit ends at its time, on its provider's no, when an issuer's back-channel logout names its sid, and at /.ferun/sign-out on the host, which ends the visitor's own.
  • At the visit's end the next navigation goes through the provider again. While the visitor's session there lives, that is a round of redirects they do not see.
  • While ferund is away the ingress goes on serving the visits it holds: a token past its end counts until its visit's end. Nobody new signs in during that time, and an ended visit is not noticed until ferund is back.
  • Not a navigation, no redirect. A script's request or an API client without a valid cookie gets 401. Only a navigation (Sec-Fetch-Mode: navigate, or a GET that accepts text/html) is sent to the provider.
  • The challenge is the ingress's own, sealed with a key from the table: the environment, the host, the path, and an end minutes away. ferund keeps nothing of it until a provider accepts, and accepts each once. The way back is the host in the seal, never one a URL names, and a code that arrives in a browser without the challenge's cookie signs nobody in.
  • A visitor nobody vouched for costs ferund nothing: no call and no row. Its first caller in a sign-in is a provider with a credential (4), and the ingress redeems only a code that carries ferund's signature.

What the app gets

  • Who the visitor is, in headers the ingress sets and takes out of every request from outside: X-FeRun-Visitor with the subject, X-FeRun-Visitor-Email where the provider gave one, and X-FeRun-Visitor-Token, the visit's token, a JWT of ferund's with the environment as its audience.
  • The headers are enough for an app that is called through the ingress alone. One that other workloads of its environment reach by a reference (Fig. 6), or that passes the identity on, checks the token against the keys published under /.ferun/.
  • The issuer's tokens go no further than the proxy. FeRun tells the app who is there; it does not hand it an access token to act for them elsewhere.

What stays as it is

  • A route's from comes first: a client outside its blocks gets 403 before anyone is asked to sign in.
  • The path /.ferun/ is the ingress's on every host; no route claims it. There the browser comes back from a provider, a visitor signs out, and the token's keys are published. ACME's HTTP-01 on port 80 is untouched.
  • The route door and attached services do not pass here: a route token still opens what it opened, and a workspace's previews stay behind their platform's own door.
  • The ingress still decides nothing about people. It holds no list and no rule, only what the table gives it: the switch per environment, the provider's address, the key for its challenges and ferund's public key.

Where it comes from

  • Ory Hydra: the challenge a login app reads and accepts over the back channel, instead of a signed answer carried through the browser.
  • Cloudflare Access: two lifetimes, a long session at the one place that knows people and a short token on each protected host, renewed without the visitor noticing; 401 for a script's request.
  • Pomerium: the session as a record at the centre that the host's cookie points to, and identity for the app as a signed assertion.
  • Ory Oathkeeper, by contrast: its cookie_session asks the session's owner on every request. That ends a session at once, and puts the owner into every request's path, which this design does not.

Left for later

  • Machine clients of a guarded environment get 401. Admission by a route token at the ingress, or a route that stays open beside guarded ones, such as a webhook's, is not decided; until then such an environment stays anyone and guards itself.
  • Environments that are public when this is built stay anyone; their tenant closes them.
  • OAuth 2.0 without OpenID Connect, where no ID token says who signed in, is not covered.

Inside ferund

One Go module (github.com/gardenlinux/feos/go), built static, with fixed dependencies: the standard library, pgx, gRPC, Protobuf, the MCP SDK, uuid, x/crypto, x/sync. The packages under go/internal/, grouped by what they do:

Sandbox engine

States, ops, placement (binpack or spread), sync and reconcile, tombstones, policies, images. Keyed tool calls outlive their caller (ADR 0023).

Environments

Deployments with plan, base, revert and tags; the controller; addons on volumes; secrets; the route door; tenants' jobs.

Fleet

Edge

Principles

  • Nodes open no port for control.They connect out to every instance of the control plane, with no broker in between.
  • No credential enters a pod.What a workload may use, feos-authn on the host adds or signs with.
  • Pods outlive their node agent.A restart or upgrade of feosd leaves the pods running, and the new feosd takes them over. On Kubernetes they are suspended and resumed for it.
  • A snapshot pins what it was written on.The Firecracker version, the CPU model and the host's kernel. Nodes keep the Firecracker versions their snapshots need.
  • PID 1 stays small.feos-host-init and feos-guest must not die; work that can fail runs in processes that can be restarted.
  • Static musl, syscalls first.No shared libraries, no shelling out to userspace tools. The one exception: mke2fs for new volumes in the guest.
  • The database's clock decides.Every lease, TTL and deadline in ferund uses PostgreSQL's now(). No node call inside a transaction.
  • What is not declared is closed.A deployment's references open the ports they name; nothing else reaches a pod, not even from its own environment.
  • FeOS knows pods.Whether a pod is an agent's sandbox or a customer's service, only FeRun knows.

Not built yet

Decided, but not built or only in part, per the ADR index and the architecture docs:

  • Images through the node (ADR 0022): the guest still pulls with the registry's token; feosd is to pull the layers and bake them into a sealed layer of diskd's.
  • Pod identity (ADR 0014): only feos-authn's HTTP side is built. The SPIFFE identity, FeID and the TLS, Kubernetes and SSH bindings are missing; the jail has no network namespace of its own yet.
  • Traffic records (ADR 0013) are made on the node but do not reach the control plane yet, so a drop does not yet name the missing reference in the console.
  • Network rules (ADR 0034): groups go to the nodes whole every 10 s rather than as acknowledged deltas; no environment-wide egress default; TCP only between workloads; no references across environments. The mesh's WireGuard keys do not rotate yet (ADR 0017).
  • Environments: no volumes for services, no MCP tools and no CLI, no addon backups, no data on a clone.
  • Ingress: no DNS-01 wildcard, no L4, no h2c.
  • Auth at the ingress (ADR 0054, proposed): visitors, access providers and ferun-auth. An environment's hosts are open to everyone until then.
  • diskd: no sweep of old layers yet, no disk size per pod.
