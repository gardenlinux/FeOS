# ADR 0030: CDNs in front of the ingress, declared on a tenant's domain

**Status:** Proposed · **Date:** 2026-11-07

A tenant may put a CDN of its own in front of a domain, and the platform
runs none: the domain declares its CDN, the CDN calls the edge with the
domain as `Host` and proves itself with a secret header the platform
made, and only a request that carries it is believed about its client's
address. The domain's certificate comes over a DNS-01 challenge
delegated by a CNAME into a zone of the platform's, before the name
points anywhere, and a domain behind a CDN counts as routed when a probe
through its name reaches the ingress. The ingress answers as shared
caches need: its own answers `no-store`, a service's answer without
`Cache-Control` and every answer with `Set-Cookie` `private`, its holds
shorter than the CDN's patience, and a purge of a site's tag after its
switch (ADR 0029). What the tenant sets at its CDN and in its DNS is a
recipe the domain's answer carries, checked line by line.

## Context

A tenant puts a CDN in front of its domain for reasons of its own: a WAF
and DDoS protection its company requires, latency for users far from the
platform's region, traffic a cache takes off its services, or because
its company's sites all sit behind one CloudFront or Cloudflare account.
Static sites (ADR 0029) are the obvious case and not the only one: a
service's pages, rendered per request, are cached by a CDN when they say
so, and an API is protected by one even when nothing of it is cached.

A CDN in front of a FeRun domain breaks it in six places:

- **The certificate.** A domain is routed when its name resolves to the
  edge's addresses (ADR 0045). Behind a CDN's proxy it resolves to the
  CDN's: it is never routed, so `ferund` never asks for its certificate,
  and HTTP-01 could not pass anyway, since a CDN that speaks HTTPS to
  its origin needs the origin's certificate before the origin can answer
  a challenge over it. A domain that was served and then moved behind a
  CDN is `dns-moved` and, after the grace, deactivated: the production
  goes off the internet three days after its owner did everything right.
- **The client's address.** The ingress sets `X-Forwarded-For` and
  `X-Real-IP` from the connection (ADR 0021), which is the CDN's edge.
  The CDN's own header with the client's address, `CF-Connecting-IP` or
  `CloudFront-Viewer-Address`, goes through to the application
  unchecked, and any client that reaches the edge directly can write it:
  an application that trusts it trusts whoever sends it.
- **The bypass.** The edge answers a domain for anyone who sends its
  name: a request that resolves the edge itself passes around the WAF
  the tenant pays the CDN for.
- **Shared caches.** The ingress's own answers (502, 503 after a hold,
  404 for an unknown host) carry no `Cache-Control`, and a CDN may keep
  an error for a while. An application's answer without `Cache-Control`
  may be cached by a CDN's heuristics, by the path's extension, which is
  how a personal `/invoice/42.pdf` reaches the next visitor.
- **Timeouts.** The ingress holds a request for minutes while a paused
  service wakes (ADR 0021); a CDN gives its origin less before it answers
  the client with an error of its own.
- **Versions.** A CDN that keeps a site's documents keeps serving blue
  after the blue-green switch (ADR 0029) until they expire.

None of this needs a CDN of the platform's. It needs the platform to
know that a domain has one, to trust what that CDN says and nothing that
only claims to be it, to get a certificate without depending on where
the name points, and to answer as a shared cache needs. `ferund` holds
no DNS credential (ADR 0045); the delegation gives it one, for a zone of
the platform's alone.

## Decision

### A domain names its CDN

- A domain (ADR 0045) may declare a CDN: a `provider` (`cloudflare`,
  `cloudfront` or `other`), `lockdown` and an optional `purge`; the
  declaration can be removed. It is the domain's and not an
  environment's: the CDN stands in front of a name, and a move of the
  domain to another environment keeps it.
- A provider is a preset of what differs between CDNs: the header that
  carries the client's address (`CF-Connecting-IP`;
  `CloudFront-Viewer-Address`, an address and a port), the headers that
  only the CDN may set (`CF-*`; `CloudFront-*`), its timeout towards the
  origin, its purge and its recipe. `other` takes the client's header by
  name and has no purge.
- The default host under the apps zone has no CDN: its DNS is the
  platform's, and a tenant's CDN stands in front of a tenant's name.

### The CDN's origin is the edge, its `Host` the domain's

- A CDN connects to the edge over HTTPS and sends the domain as the
  request's `Host` and as SNI. The ingress serves the domain as it
  serves any: its certificate, its table entry, its environment's
  routes. The application sees its own host, builds its own URLs and
  sets its own cookies, behind a CDN as without.
- So the origin needs the domain's own certificate, publicly trusted:
  CloudFront, told to pass the viewer's `Host`, checks the origin's
  certificate against that host; Cloudflare's `Full (strict)` checks it
  against the requested host. A CDN that checks nothing is not a mode
  the recipe offers.
- **Cookies work as without a CDN.** The browser sees only the domain; a
  CDN passes `Set-Cookie` and `Cookie` through, and the browser keeps a
  cookie for the host it asked, whatever the origin's name. What needs
  the application to know the domain, a cookie's `Domain`, a redirect,
  an absolute URL, an OAuth redirect URI, a CSRF check of `Origin`,
  learns it from `Host` and `X-Forwarded-Host`; `Secure` follows
  `X-Forwarded-Proto`, which is `https`, since the CDN reaches the edge
  over HTTPS only. A tenant's domain keeps its cookies' `Domain`; only
  hosts under the zones have it removed (ADR 0021), and those have no
  CDN.
- **A wrong `Host` fails loudly.** A CDN that sends its origin's name
  instead reaches no environment: the edge's own name serves nothing,
  the request gets 404, and the probe names the cause. No application is
  ever served under a host its users do not see, so none sets a cookie
  for the wrong one.
- The ingress takes nothing else from the CDN but the request: no
  configuration, no callback, no account of the tenant's.

### The certificate comes over a delegated DNS-01

- A domain's certificate may come over DNS-01 through a delegation: the
  tenant sets `_acme-challenge.<name>` as a CNAME to a name per claim in
  a zone of the platform's, and `ferund` answers the challenge with a
  TXT record there, through the DNS provider of that zone, with the one
  DNS credential it holds, for that zone alone. Let's Encrypt follows
  the CNAME.
- This holds for any domain, with a CDN or without: a delegated domain
  gets its certificate before its name points anywhere, the cutover
  without a gap. Its gate is the CNAME, checked at the zone's servers as
  every record is (ADR 0045), in place of the routing: `ferund` spends
  no order on a domain whose delegation is missing.
- The label is the claim's own, made at the claim: a tenant's CNAME lets
  `ferund` answer for that claim and no other, and a claim superseded or
  deleted takes its label along.
- A domain without the CNAME keeps HTTP-01 (ADR 0045), and a domain
  behind a CDN without it gets no certificate; the claim's answer says
  so. HTTP-01 through a CDN cannot pass: Let's Encrypt follows the CDN's
  redirect to HTTPS, and a CDN that checks its origin's certificate
  reaches no origin that lacks one.
- One place where the CNAME may collide: Cloudflare validates the
  certificates it serves at its own edge with TXT records at
  `_acme-challenge.<host>` in the tenant's zone. Its universal
  certificate covers the apex and a wildcard below it, so a first-level
  name (`shop.example.com`) is validated at the apex's label and meets
  no CNAME. The apex itself, and a name with an advanced certificate of
  its own, would have both at one label, which DNS does not allow beside
  a CNAME.

### The CDN proves itself with a secret header

- A domain with a CDN has an **origin secret**, random, made by
  `ferund`, sealed as secrets are (ADR 0044), sent in the ingress's
  table and shown to its tenant. The tenant's CDN adds it to every
  request towards the origin as the header `FeRun-Origin-Secret`, a
  fixed header every CDN can add (a CloudFront origin's custom header, a
  Cloudflare request header rule). A rotation makes a new secret beside
  the old, both valid until the tenant drops the old.
- A request for the domain **with** the secret came through the domain's
  CDN: the client's address is the one the provider's header carries,
  and becomes `X-Real-IP` and the start of `X-Forwarded-For`, the CDN's
  edge after it; the provider's headers pass on (country, protocol, its
  request ID); the secret is removed before the request goes on.
- A request **without** it, for any host, has its client's address from
  the connection, and the headers of every provider the ingress knows
  are dropped, on every host and not only on CDN domains, as `Forwarded`
  and `True-Client-IP` are (ADR 0021): `CF-Connecting-IP`,
  `CF-IPCountry`, `CloudFront-Viewer-Address` and the like are what a
  client writes when it wants an application to believe it.
- With `lockdown`, a request for the domain without the secret gets 403
  with `no-store`: no request passes around the tenant's CDN. It is off
  by default and switched on by the tenant once its CDN is in place,
  which the probe shows; turned on before the DNS points at the CDN, it
  would refuse every client still on the direct path.
- Not the CDN's address ranges: they show that a request came through
  Cloudflare or CloudFront, not through this tenant's account, and any
  customer of the same CDN can point its own configuration at the edge
  and send a domain's name.

### A domain behind a CDN is routed when a probe through its name passes

- DNS cannot show that a CDN's origin is the edge: the name resolves to
  the CDN, and the origin is in the CDN's configuration. So `ferund`
  asks the path itself: a `GET` of a well-known path of the platform's
  under the domain, with a nonce, through the public internet. Every
  instance answers the probe's nonces from its table, as it answers ACME
  challenges, on every host, with what it saw: the host, whether the
  secret came and was right, the client's header and the protocol, under
  `no-store`. The probe passes when it arrives at the domain's host with
  the secret.
- A domain with a CDN is **routed** when its probe passes, in place of
  ADR 0045's addresses of the edge; the hourly checks, the quarter-hour
  checks while it is wrong and the grace work as they do. A probe that
  fails on the way, a timeout or the CDN's own error page, is a failed
  lookup and changes nothing; one answered without the secret, at
  another host, or not by the ingress at all is a wrong answer. A probe
  answered at the edge's own name tells the tenant that its CDN does not
  send the domain as `Host`.
- A domain is in the ingress's table once it has a certificate, so that
  the CDN can be set up and probed before the name moves; it is served,
  and takes its environment's default host away (ADR 0045), only once it
  is routed, directly or through its CDN.
- A served domain that moves behind a CDN declares the CDN, gets the
  delegation, and then moves its name: it is routed by the probe from
  then on, and never `dns-moved`.

### What the ingress sends shared caches

- **Its own answers** carry `Cache-Control: no-store`, on every host:
  the 404 of an unknown host or path, a 502, a 503 after a hold or at a
  limit, the redirect from port 80, the 403 of a lockdown, a probe's and
  a challenge's answer. Cloudflare keeps none of them; CloudFront keeps
  a 404 and a 5xx for its error caching time whatever the origin says,
  so the recipe sets that time to 0.
- **A service's answer without `Cache-Control`**, on a domain with a
  CDN, gets `Cache-Control: private`, so that no shared cache keeps it
  by a heuristic: Cloudflare caches by the path's extension for two
  hours when the origin says nothing, and that is how a personal
  `/export/42.csv` reaches the next visitor. A browser treats the answer
  as it treated it without the header. What an application says itself
  passes untouched: `public`, `s-maxage`, `CDN-Cache-Control`,
  `Surrogate-Control`, `stale-while-revalidate`, `stale-if-error`,
  `Vary`. Which of its answers a shared cache may keep is the
  application's to know and to say; the platform only keeps the cache
  from guessing. The recipe asks for a CDN that obeys the origin:
  CloudFront's `CachingOptimized` keeps everything for at least a
  second, `private` and `no-store` included, and is not what it names.
- **An answer that sets a cookie is never a shared cache's.** On a
  domain with a CDN, an answer with `Set-Cookie` gets `Cache-Control:
  private`, also where the application said `public` or `s-maxage`:
  CloudFront, where it forwards cookies, caches the object with its
  `Set-Cookie` and hands one visitor's session cookie to the next;
  Cloudflare does so under "cache everything" with an edge TTL. Here the
  platform overrides the application, because a cached `Set-Cookie` is
  always a mistake and the one that costs most. The record of such an
  answer says it was overridden.
- **A site's answers** (ADR 0029) keep their defaults, per host:
  `public, max-age=0, must-revalidate` with strong ETags. Without a
  purge a CDN gains little from them: Cloudflare does not keep an answer
  with `max-age=0`, and CloudFront revalidates it only on paths where it
  forwards no cookies; hashed assets are what it keeps, by the site's
  `immutable` rule. Where the domain has a purge, the default gains
  `s-maxage` (the site's `cdnTtl`, an hour by default), which both CDNs
  honour and browsers ignore: the CDN serves documents too without
  asking for that long, and the purge after a switch ends it. A headers
  rule of the site wins over both, as for every default.

### A purge follows the switch

- A domain's `purge` names a credential of the tenant's (ADR 0033) that
  holds the CDN's API key, allowed to purge its zone or invalidate its
  distribution and nothing more, and what to purge in. A rotation of the
  credential reaches the purge without a change of the domain.
- **By tag.** On a domain with a purge, the ingress tags every answer of
  a site or a routed service with the object it came from, by the
  environment's ID and the object's name, so that a purge after a rename
  (ADR 0018) still reaches what was cached before it, in Cloudflare's
  `Cache-Tag`, which Cloudflare removes before the visitor, or in the
  header the CloudFront distribution names for its tags. After a site's
  switch (ADR 0029) `ferund` purges that site's tag: its files, and not
  the API beside it on the same host. `purgeOn` adds a deployment of a
  routed service, for pages a service renders and the CDN keeps. Where
  tags are not set up, a purge takes the route's prefix.
- The switch does not wait for the purge: the origin is right at once,
  and the CDN follows within its purge's time. A purge that fails is
  retried with backoff, and after an hour it is an incident on the
  environment with what the CDN answered; the site's status shows the
  purge's state.
- This is the one call `ferund` makes to a CDN, with a credential the
  tenant gives for it alone. CloudFront's is signed with SigV4, as the
  object store client's calls are (ADR 0029).

### Timeouts

- On a domain with a CDN, a request held for a waking service
  (ADR 0021) is answered before the CDN gives up on the origin: the
  hold's bound is a few seconds below the CDN's timeout, and the answer
  is 503 with `Retry-After` and `no-store`. Cloudflare waits 125 s for
  an answer; CloudFront 30 s by default and at most 120 s without a
  quota request, which the recipe asks for. A production behind a CDN is
  better not paused at all, and the recipe says so.
- **Idle connections: the CDN must close first.** A CDN that reuses an
  origin connection the ingress has just closed answers that request
  with an error of its own. CloudFront's keep-alive can be set, and the
  recipe sets it below the ingress's idle timeout (ADR 0021). Cloudflare
  reuses idle connections for up to 900 s and lets nobody change that,
  so the ingress keeps a connection on which a request carried a valid
  secret idle for longer than that; every other connection keeps the
  ingress's bound.

### The recipe: what the tenant sets, as data, and checked

- FeRun does not configure a tenant's CDN. Its account, its rules and
  its other domains are the tenant's, and an API key that could change
  them does not belong in the platform. Instead, a domain's answer
  carries a **recipe**: the DNS records and the CDN's settings, each a
  name and a value, for the declared provider, with the claim's own
  values filled in (the secret, the delegation's target). FeCode shows
  it as a list to tick, an agent reads it as data.
- What a recipe names, for every provider: the claim's TXT record
  (ADR 0045), the delegation's CNAME, the name to the CDN, the edge as
  the origin over HTTPS only with a certificate check, `Host` passed on,
  the secret header, the client's header, caching by the origin's
  `Cache-Control`, HTTP redirected to HTTPS at the CDN, and the origin's
  timeout and keep-alive.
- **Cloudflare:** the name as a proxied A and AAAA record with the
  edge's addresses, not a CNAME to the edge's name, since a proxied CNAME
  into a zone of another Cloudflare account fails and the platform's
  zone may well be one; SSL `Full (strict)`; a request header rule that
  sets the secret; for the purge, a token allowed to purge the zone.
- **CloudFront:** the domain as an alternate name of the distribution,
  with a viewer certificate from ACM, which the tenant gets, validated at
  its own label beside the delegation; an origin request policy that
  passes `Host` and the viewer's address; a cache policy with a minimum
  TTL of 0 that takes the TTL from the origin, without cookies on a
  site's paths so that CloudFront revalidates there, and with them on a
  service's; the secret as an origin custom header; error caching at 0;
  the response timeout at its maximum and a keep-alive below the
  ingress's idle timeout; the tags' header for the purge and a key
  allowed to invalidate; an apex only through Route 53's alias or
  CloudFront's static addresses, since other providers' flattening is
  not documented for it.
- The checks say what holds: the records, the probe and what it saw
  (secret, host, client's header), the purge's last answer, and how many
  requests reached the domain without the secret in the last hour, so
  that the tenant sees when `lockdown` can go on.

### Records

- A request through a CDN is recorded with the client's address from the
  CDN's header, and beside it the CDN's provider and the edge's address
  that connected (ADR 0021, ADR 0026); a request with a wrong secret is
  recorded as such. The traffic per service is unchanged: a request is a
  request, wherever it was cached before.

## Alternatives considered

- **The CDN's address ranges as the proof**, Cloudflare's published
  list, CloudFront's origin-facing prefix list: they prove the CDN, not
  the tenant's account in it.
- **Authenticated origin pulls**, a client certificate the CDN presents
  to the origin: Cloudflare's global certificate is every customer's and
  proves Cloudflare alone; a zone's own certificate proves the tenant,
  but it is one CDN's feature, while a fixed header is what every CDN
  can add. It can come later as a second proof beside the secret.
- **An origin host per environment** under the platform's wildcard,
  which the CDN calls with that host: no certificate per domain, and an
  application that sees a host its users never see, with wrong
  redirects, wrong absolute URLs, and a `Host` that `X-Forwarded-Host`
  has to correct in every framework.
- **HTTP-01 through the CDN**: a CDN that checks its origin's
  certificate cannot reach an origin without one, and one that does not
  check gives the tenant no TLS worth the name.
- **Certificates the tenant brings**, such as Cloudflare's origin CA:
  long-lived, one provider's, and a renewal nobody watches; the
  delegation makes them unnecessary for CDNs.
- **FeRun configures the CDN** through the providers' APIs: wide
  credentials in the platform, an API per provider that changes on its
  own schedule, and a tenant's CDN that FeRun and its owner both write.
- **A CDN of the platform's** in front of the edge for every tenant,
  such as Cloudflare for SaaS: one provider in every request's path,
  which ADR 0021 declined; an operator may still put one there, and the
  same secret then proves it.
- **Leave it to the applications**: each would have to know its CDN's
  header, a way to tell a real one from a forged one, and nothing of the
  ingress's own answers.

## Consequences

- A tenant puts its CDN in front of a production in three steps,
  declare, delegate, move the name, and the production never leaves the
  internet for it: the certificate is there before the name moves, and
  the domain is routed by the probe from then on.
- An application behind a CDN sees its real client, its own host and its
  own cookies, and nothing a client forged: the providers' headers reach
  no application without the secret, on any domain.
- `ferund` holds one DNS credential, for the delegation zone alone, and
  per purging domain a credential the tenant gives; it configures no
  CDN. Operators delegate a zone for ACME to a DNS provider `ferund` can
  write to, with a token for that zone alone.
- The ingress's own answers are never kept by a shared cache, on any
  host; on a CDN domain nothing of an application is cached that it did
  not ask to have cached, and a cached `Set-Cookie` cannot happen. A
  waking service behind a CDN answers 503 before the CDN does, and a
  production behind a CDN should not pause.
- A site's switch reaches the CDN's cache within the purge's time where
  the tenant gave a purge credential, else within the site's lifetimes.
- FeCode's domain dialog gains the CDN: the provider, the recipe as a
  list to tick with the checks beside each line, the secret, and
  `lockdown` once the probe passes.

## Open questions

- **Cloudflare at an apex.** Whether Cloudflare's own validation still
  passes with the delegation's CNAME at the apex's `_acme-challenge`,
  where DNS allows no TXT record beside it, is not documented; it is
  tested in a zone of our own before the recipe recommends the
  delegation for an apex or a name with an advanced certificate of its
  own. If not, the fallback is a certificate the tenant brings
  (Cloudflare's origin CA), which ADR 0045's backlog keeps.

## History

Decided on 2026-11-07 and committed on branch `static-sites` on
2026-11-08 (53d9121), after ADR 0029 (40fc62b). Brought to the main
line on 2026-11-08 as Proposed; nothing of it is built. The original
leaned on a DNS interface of ADR 0021's draft, meant for the zones'
wildcards over DNS-01; ADR 0045, split from ADR 0021 on 2026-11-08,
records that it was never built and that `ferund` holds no DNS
credential, so the delegation here is `ferund`'s first DNS-01 and its one
DNS credential. The text departs from ADR 0045 in two places, which it
names: for a delegated domain the CNAME gates the certificate in place of
the routing, and a domain behind a CDN is routed by the probe in place
of the edge's addresses.
